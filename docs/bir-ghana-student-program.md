Prepared for: Black in Rehab leadership
Prepared by: Edquity at the Margins
Date: 21 August 2026
Status: working draft for the strategy session

---

## 1. The question, restated

You asked what needs to exist for universities to feel confident sending their students into
this program. The answer is narrower and more practical than a curriculum. A university
fieldwork office does not evaluate the quality of your program in the first instance, because
it cannot. It evaluates whether the placement satisfies the accreditation standard it is
audited against, whether the supervisor holds the credential the standard requires, and whether
the risk office can approve the travel. A beautiful curriculum with an ineligible supervisor
gets declined. A modest curriculum with a documented supervisor, a signed affiliation
agreement, and a credible emergency plan gets approved.

So the design work splits into two streams that move at the same time. Stream one is the
compliance and partnership file, which determines whether you can sell the program at all.
Stream two is the educational design, which determines whether it is worth buying. This
document treats stream one first, because it contains the constraints that shape stream two.

---

## 2. The compliance and partnership file

This is the single most important thing to build, and most of it is documentation rather than
design. Assemble it once, thoroughly, and five university negotiations become five reviews of
the same packet rather than five redesigns.

### 2.1 The binding constraint: supervisor eligibility

The credential of the on site supervisor determines what the placement can be, and it is the
constraint most likely to invalidate an otherwise sound plan.

For occupational therapy, accreditation standards require that Level II fieldwork be supervised
by an occupational therapist with a defined minimum of practice experience, and placements
outside the United States carry additional requirements regarding the supervisor's qualification
in that country, typically tied to graduation from a program approved by the World Federation of
Occupational Therapists. Ghana's occupational therapy workforce is small, so supervisor
eligibility cannot be assumed and must be verified person by person before any hours are sold.
Where no eligible in country supervisor exists for a given cohort, your options are to place a
qualified supervisor in country for the duration, to arrange a documented co-supervision
structure that the university's fieldwork office accepts in writing, or to route those students
to the capstone pathway rather than the fieldwork pathway.

For speech language pathology the constraint is harder. Clinical clock hours counted toward
certification require supervision by a clinician holding the Certificate of Clinical Competence
in speech language pathology. Unless you have a certified supervisor on site or travelling with
the cohort, speech language pathology students should be offered the capstone pathway, and the
materials should say so plainly rather than leave the university to discover it.

For physical therapy, clinical education experiences are governed by the relevant accreditation
standards and by state licensure rules that vary, so the practical route is to ask each
partner's director of clinical education what their program requires of an international site
and to build to the strictest answer you receive.

I am summarising these requirements in substance rather than quoting standard numbers, and you
should not rely on my summary in a partnership conversation. Assign one person to obtain the
current standard language directly from each accreditor and from each partner's fieldwork or
clinical education office, in writing, and to keep that file current. This is a two week task
and it protects everything downstream.

### 2.2 Ghanaian regulatory clearance

Before students provide any hands on service, you need documented answers to the following,
obtained from Ghanaian authorities rather than inferred:

Whether foreign trainees require registration or written permission from the Allied Health
Professions Council of Ghana, and under what conditions. Whether the placement facilities
require their own approval from the Ghana Health Service or their governing body. What visa
category applies to a student on an unpaid clinical training placement, and what documentation
the students must carry. Whether any data collection, including quality improvement work or
capstone projects involving patient records, requires review by a Ghanaian institutional review
board. What professional liability coverage is recognised locally, and whether the students'
university coverage extends to Ghana.

Universities will ask for these in writing. Having them ready is the difference between a
partnership conversation and a compliance investigation.

## 3. Pathways

Three pathways, clearly separated, because the accreditation posture, the supervision
requirement, and the evaluation instrument all differ.

**Pathway A, Clinical Fieldwork.** Credit bearing supervised clinical hours, evaluated on the
university's required instrument, supervised by a clinician meeting that discipline's standard.
Measured in supervised hours and in competency ratings. This pathway carries the highest
compliance burden and the highest value to students, and it is available only where supervisor
eligibility is documented. Within occupational therapy, offer a short observational placement
of two to three weeks and a full placement of twelve weeks as separate products, since the
supervision requirements and student capability differ substantially.

**Pathway B, Capstone and Doctoral Experiential Component.** For clinical doctorate students
completing a capstone, doctoral experiential component, or equivalent. The student delivers a
project with a defined product, mentored by a faculty mentor at the university and an on site
mentor in Ghana. Measured in deliverables and in scholarly output rather than in clinical hours.
The compliance burden is lower because the student is not providing supervised clinical service
as the primary activity, and the value to Ghanaian partners is higher because the product stays
behind. Duration of eight to sixteen weeks.

**Pathway C, Global Rehabilitation Immersion.** Non credit or elective credit, two to three
weeks, observational and community based, no independent clinical contact. This is the entry
product for students whose programs will not approve A or B, and for early students who are not
yet clinically ready. It is also the pathway that most needs an explicit anti-tourism design,
because short observational trips are where extractive dynamics concentrate.

State the differences in a single comparison table in the prospectus, and state plainly which
pathway generates clinical hours and which does not. Ambiguity on that point is the fastest way
to lose a university partner.

## 4. Program architecture

Five phases, with a readiness gate between phases two and three.

**Phase 1, Recruitment and selection, twelve to sixteen weeks before departure.** Application,
including a statement addressing why this program rather than a domestic placement, and a
reference from a faculty member. Interview. Confirmation from the university that the placement
is approved and that credit will be granted. Deposit and agreement signature.

**Phase 2, Pre-departure preparation, eight weeks before departure to one week before.** See
section 6. Ends with a readiness gate.

**Phase 3, Arrival and orientation, days one to five in country.** No clinical contact until
orientation is complete, and this should be a stated rule rather than a practice. Orientation
covers safety and logistics, site tours, introductions to Ghanaian staff by name and role,
language practice in context, the health system briefing, and the scope of practice
conversation held in the room where the work will happen.

**Phase 4, Placement, weeks one through six, ten, or twelve depending on pathway.** See
section 5.

**Phase 5, Reentry and dissemination, two weeks and eight weeks after return.** Two structured
sessions, a final reflective product, a presentation to the student's university if the
university will host one, and induction into the BIR alumni network.

The readiness gate at the end of Phase 2 matters more than it looks. Publish the criteria in
advance: completion of all pre-departure modules, a passing score on the safety and scope
assessment, documented immunisations and insurance, visa in hand, and completion of the
positionality assignment. A student who does not clear the gate does not travel, and the refund
schedule should reflect that possibility honestly.

## 5. Weekly structure

Fix the rhythm and publish it, because a predictable week is what allows supervision, reflection,
and capstone work to actually happen rather than getting displaced by clinical demand.

| Day | Structure |
| --- | --- |
| Monday | Morning goal setting huddle, thirty minutes, student states the week's clinical and learning goals. Clinical day follows |
| Tuesday | Clinical day |
| Wednesday | Clinical day. One hour individual supervision with the on site supervisor, scheduled and protected |
| Thursday | Clinical day |
| Friday | Non clinical learning day: didactic session, documentation, capstone work, and the ninety minute group reflection seminar |

Weekly requirements regardless of pathway: one written journal entry against a supplied prompt,
submitted before the Friday seminar so the facilitator can read it; one hour of individual
supervision, documented on a supervision log the university can inspect; and attendance at the
group seminar. Monthly requirements: a written self assessment against the competency framework,
and a supervisor rating against the same framework, discussed together.

Progression of responsibility across a twelve week placement, expressed as entrustment rather
than as hours:

| Weeks | Expected level | What the student does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 to 2 | Observation with explanation | Shadows, builds an occupational and contextual profile of the setting, documents under direct review |
| 3 to 4 | Assisted practice | Carries two to three learners or patients with direct supervision present, drafts all documentation for co-signature. Midterm evaluation at end of week 4 or 6 |
| 5 to 8 | Supervised independence | Caseload grows to a level the supervisor sets, supervision becomes indirect and available rather than present, student runs their own sessions |
| 9 to 11 | Entrusted within scope | Full agreed caseload, independent documentation with review, contributes to a site process or protocol |
| 12 | Handoff | Final evaluation, written handoff of every case to the site, partner feedback collected, closing reflection |

Shorten proportionally for six week placements, and hold the principle that the first fifteen
percent of any placement is observational.

## 6. Pre-departure curriculum

Six modules, delivered as three live sessions plus asynchronous work, totalling roughly twenty
to twenty five hours. Each module has an assessment, and the assessments together constitute
the readiness gate.

**Module 1, Ghana in context.** History, political and economic context, the structure of the
health and education systems, how rehabilitation services are financed and accessed, the
disability rights context and relevant Ghanaian legislation, and the actual availability of
occupational therapy, physical therapy, and speech language services. Taught by Ghanaian
faculty. Assessment: a short written brief on how a family in the placement catchment would
access rehabilitation services, with sources.

**Module 2, Language and communication.** Functional Twi or the language of the placement site,
enough for greeting, courtesy, orientation, and basic clinical instruction. Interpreter use, and
specifically how to work with a colleague who is interpreting rather than treating them as a
translation device. Assessment: a recorded five minute functional exchange.

**Module 3, Positionality and power.** Where the student sits in relation to the community they
are entering, including nationality, education, wealth, and, for the many students who share
African ancestry with their hosts, the specific and non-simple relationship between diasporic
identity and local belonging. This module is where the anti-saviorism work happens, and it is
substantive rather than attitudinal: what extractive global health practice looks like
concretely, what a student's presence costs a host site in supervision time and clinic
throughput, and what the ethics literature says about short term training experiences (Crump &
Sugarman, 2008; Crump et al., 2010). Assessment: a written positionality statement, revised once
after feedback.

**Module 4, Scope, ethics, and safety.** What the student may and may not do, why the boundary
exists legally and ethically, and how to decline a request to exceed scope when the person asking
outranks them. The health and safety file, the emergency action plan, and the incident procedure.
Assessment: a scenario based assessment with a pass threshold, covering scope violations, a
medical emergency, a safeguarding concern, and a photography request. This module requires a pass
to travel.

**Module 5, Clinical preparation for a resource constrained setting.** Practising without the
assessment tools, equipment, and reimbursement structures the student trained inside. Improvised
and locally sourced materials. Assessment without standardised norms for the population. Working
with caregivers as the primary intervention agent. Documentation with limited records
infrastructure. Assessment: a written plan for evaluating a described case using observation and
caregiver interview alone.

**Module 6, Learning agreement.** The student writes three to five personal learning objectives
mapped to the BIR competency domains and to their university's required objectives, agreed by
the faculty mentor and the on site supervisor before departure. This becomes the document
supervision is organised around, and it converts a placement into a program.

## 7. Cultural preparation as a strand, not a session

Two design choices carry almost all of the value here.

**Ghanaian colleagues teach it, and they are paid to teach it.** Cultural preparation delivered
by American faculty about Ghana is a briefing. Cultural preparation delivered by Ghanaian
clinicians and educators, who appear as named faculty in the program materials and receive
honoraria, is an education. This is also the most visible signal to a university that the
partnership is real.

**Intercultural competence is assessed as an outcome, not assumed from exposure.** Time in
country does not reliably produce intercultural competence, and the developmental models in the
field treat it as a set of attitudes, knowledge, and skills that produce observable internal and
external outcomes (Deardorff, 2006). Build a rubric with four domains, assess at three points,
pre-departure, midpoint, and reentry, using a combination of self assessment, supervisor
observation, and the reflective writing. Do not use a satisfaction survey as a proxy.

The strand runs across all five phases: Modules 1 through 3 pre-departure, the in country
orientation, a recurring theme in the Friday seminar, a specific competency domain in the
evaluation framework, and a dedicated reentry session, because reentry is where most of the
consolidation actually happens and where unprocessed experience turns into either insight or a
story about how grateful the locals were.

## 8. Clinical and community experiences

Design each placement as a portfolio rather than a single setting, so students see how services
connect and so no single site absorbs the whole supervision burden.

A defensible mix over twelve weeks: a primary clinical site accounting for roughly sixty percent
of time, where the student's caseload and supervision live; a school or community based setting
for roughly twenty percent, which is where most of the disability population actually is; a
community or home visit component for roughly ten percent, conducted with local staff and never
alone; and roughly ten percent structured non clinical learning, covering the health system,
policy, and the capstone work.

Two rules worth stating explicitly in the materials. Students never conduct home visits or
community outreach unaccompanied by local staff, for safety and because the local relationship
belongs to the local clinician. Students do not initiate or lead any activity the site has not
requested, which is the operational form of the anti-saviorism commitment.

## 9. Reflection, mentorship, and professional development

Reflection is the mechanism that turns experience into learning, and it needs structure to work
rather than good intentions (Kolb, 1984; Schön, 1983).

**Structured reflection.** A weekly written entry against a rotating prompt, roughly four hundred
words, submitted before the Friday seminar. Prompts should push toward the specific and the
uncomfortable rather than the general: describe a moment you did not know what to do; describe
something you assumed that turned out to be wrong; describe something a local colleague does
better than you were taught to; describe a resource you would have reached for at home and what
was used instead. The seminar discusses entries with consent, and the facilitator's job is to
keep the group from settling into either despair or gratitude narratives.

**Mentorship, three relationships and each with a defined job.** The on site supervisor owns
clinical competence and the formal evaluation. The BIR program mentor, a Black rehabilitation
professional who is not the supervisor, owns professional identity and career development, meets
the student every two weeks, and continues for six months after return. The university faculty
mentor owns academic requirements and credit. Write down which one a student takes a given
problem to, because unclear escalation is how problems go unraised.

**Professional development strand.** Four sessions across the placement: global and community
rehabilitation as a career, practising and leading as a Black rehabilitation professional,
translating this experience into interviews and applications without exoticising it, and
scholarship and dissemination including how to write up a capstone honestly. Deliverable: a
professional development plan and a written artifact, either a capstone product, a case report,
or a conference abstract.

The mentorship arrangement is where your differentiation is most concrete, and section 13
returns to it.

## 10. Student evaluation and competency tracking

Five universities will require up to five different evaluation instruments. Trying to harmonise
them is a losing effort, and abandoning your own framework leaves you unable to report program
level outcomes. The structural answer is two layers.

**Layer one, the university's instrument, summative and authoritative.** Whatever each partner
requires, completed by the eligible supervisor at the required intervals, and returned through
the required channel. This is the record of record.

**Layer two, the BIR competency framework, formative and comparable.** Your own framework,
completed monthly by student and supervisor independently and then discussed, used for
formative feedback during the placement and aggregated across cohorts and universities for
program level reporting. This is what lets you tell partner six what outcomes to expect.

Six domains, which cover the ground the university instruments do not:

1. Culturally responsive and context appropriate practice
2. Clinical reasoning under resource constraint
3. Professional behaviour and communication across difference
4. Collaboration with local personnel, families, and community structures
5. Ethical practice, scope, and self limitation
6. Reflective practice and self directed learning

Rate each domain on an entrustment scale rather than a numeric quality scale, because entrustment
language tells a supervisor what decision to make rather than asking for an abstract judgment
(ten Cate, 2005):

| Level | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Observes only |
| 2 | Acts with the supervisor present and directing |
| 3 | Acts with the supervisor present and available |
| 4 | Acts independently within agreed scope, with review afterwards |
| 5 | Acts independently and supports a peer |

Write three to four behavioural anchors per domain per level, which is the writing task that
makes the framework usable. Organise the whole assessment picture along the classic competence
progression, from knows, to knows how, to shows how, to does in practice (Miller, 1990), and
make sure at least one instrument sits at the does level, which in practice means direct
observation and chart review rather than self report.

**Concern and removal procedure, written before the first cohort.** Define the behavioural
thresholds that trigger a formal concern, the remediation process, the thresholds that trigger
removal from the placement, who makes the decision, what the site is told, what the university
is told and within what period, and what happens to the student's credit and fees. Universities
will ask for this document, and the honest reason to have it is that you will eventually need it.

## 11. University partnership requirements

Five named partners: Nova Southeastern University, Florida A&M University, Gannon University at
the Ruskin campus, Howard University, and Xavier University. Several are historically Black
institutions, which aligns directly with BIR's mission and is a genuine strategic asset in
recruitment and in fundraising narrative.

Practical approach. Do not approach all five at once. Choose one anchor partner, ideally one
where you already have a relationship with the fieldwork coordinator or clinical education
director, and build the full packet against their requirements. Their requirements will be
stricter and more specific than anything you would generate unprompted, and satisfying them
produces a packet that mostly satisfies the others. Run the first cohort small, two to four
students from the anchor partner, treat it explicitly as a pilot, and collect evaluation data
you can show to partners two through five.

The person you actually need in each institution is the academic fieldwork coordinator for
occupational therapy, the director of clinical education for physical therapy, or the clinic
director for speech language pathology, together with whoever runs education abroad or
international risk management. The department faculty member enthusiastic about the idea cannot
approve it, and building the relationship only with them is the common failure mode.

Per partner, you need a tracked file containing: the accreditation standard language they apply
to international placements, their required evaluation instrument, their required site specific
objectives format, their affiliation agreement template, their insurance and immunisation
requirements, their travel approval process and any country restrictions, their credit and hours
requirements, and the named contacts with dates of last contact. Build this as a simple tracker
rather than an email archive.

## 12. Value for Ghanaian partners

You named this as a question, which is the right instinct, and it is the dimension on which most
international placement programs quietly fail. Student presence has a real cost to a host site,
paid in supervision hours, in interpretation, in slower clinic throughput, and in the attention
of senior clinicians who are already scarce. A program that does not price and repay that cost is
extracting value while describing itself as partnership. The ethics guidance in this area is
explicit that host institutions should benefit and that the burden on them should be assessed
rather than assumed (Crump et al., 2010).

Eight mechanisms, all of which are contractual or accounted rather than aspirational:

**Pay the partners.** Supervision stipends and site fees written into the student fee, disclosed
to universities as a line item, and paid on a stated schedule regardless of cohort size. Publish
the percentage of the student fee that reaches Ghanaian individuals and institutions.

**Partner defined capstone topics.** Every Pathway B project comes from a list the Ghanaian
partners write. Students choose from that list rather than proposing their own interest, which is
the single cheapest structural change available and the one that most changes who the work serves.

**Retained assets.** Every cohort leaves something the site keeps and can use without BIR: a
completed protocol, a translated caregiver resource, a trained staff member, a piece of
equipment, a documented process. Name the asset in advance and verify it at handoff.

**Reciprocal learning flow.** Fund Ghanaian clinicians to attend BIR continuing education at no
cost, and build a pathway, even a small one initially, for Ghanaian clinicians or students to
undertake an observership or a training experience in the United States. A one directional flow
of students is not an exchange, and naming that honestly is better than the alternative.

**Co-authorship, agreed in advance.** Any publication, conference presentation, or public
write-up names Ghanaian partners as authors or presenters, with authorship order and
contributions agreed in writing before the work starts rather than negotiated after.

**Partner advisory board with real authority.** Ghanaian partners hold a formal decision role,
including the ability to decline a placement, to cap student numbers, and to pause the program at
a site. Authority that can only advise is not authority.

**Capacity matched cohort sizing.** Cohort size is set by the supervision capacity the sites
state they have, not by student demand. Write this rule down, because the pressure to exceed it
will be commercial and constant.

**Annual net benefit accounting.** Publish, once a year, what the program earned and what
partners received in cash, training, equipment, and assets, alongside a partner satisfaction
measure collected by someone who does not work for BIR. This is unusual, it is verifiable, and it
will do more for your credibility with universities and funders than any other single document.

## 13. What makes this different from an international placement

Four differences, in the order a prospective student or partner would find them persuasive.

**Supervision and mentorship by Black rehabilitation professionals, inside a program built by
Black rehabilitation professionals.** No generic placement provider offers this, and for many
students it will be the first clinical experience in which their supervisor and their mentor
share their professional and racial experience. Say this plainly in the materials rather than
implying it, and structure it deliberately through the BIR program mentor role and the
professional development strand.

**A curriculum with assessed competencies, not a placement with a journal.** Pre-departure
modules with a readiness gate, a fixed weekly structure, a competency framework with entrustment
levels, three assessment points, and a reentry phase. Most international placements are a site
plus a supervisor plus goodwill.

**Reciprocity that is contractual and audited.** Section 12 turns partner benefit from a claim
into a set of documents and numbers. This is the part that a university's global engagement
office will actually recognise as unusual.

**A cohort and a network rather than an individual trip.** Students travel together, reflect
together, return together, and stay connected to BIR afterwards, which turns the placement into
an entry point to a professional community rather than a line on a résumé.

Notice that three of the four are structural rather than experiential. The experience of being in
Ghana is not the differentiator, since anyone can arrange that. The design around it is.

## 16. Risks, and the things that go wrong

| Risk | Mitigation |
| --- | --- |
| No eligible supervisor for a discipline, invalidating the fieldwork pathway | Verify supervisor eligibility per discipline before marketing. Route affected disciplines to the capstone pathway and say so in the materials |
| A student exceeds scope, whether from enthusiasm or from being asked | Scenario based assessment with a pass requirement, a written scope statement posted at each site, and a stated procedure for declining a request from a senior person |
| A serious medical or security incident | Emergency action plan, medical evacuation insurance, twenty four hour contact, a named in country coordinator, and a documented rehearsal during orientation |
| Photographs of patients appear on social media | Explicit written prohibition, consequences stated, and the reasoning taught in Module 3 rather than issued as a rule |
| A site withdraws mid cohort | Portfolio placement design with more sites than any single cohort needs, plus a contingency plan naming the fallback |
| Supervision burden exceeds what partners can carry | Capacity matched cohort sizing, supervision stipends, and a partner veto that actually functions |
| The program reads as voluntourism to a university global engagement office | Section 12 documented and published, and the pilot outcomes report leading with partner benefit |
| Cash flow, since costs precede fees | Deposit schedule, and a decision rule on the minimum cohort size below which a cohort does not run |
| A student needs to be removed | Written concern and removal procedure agreed with each university before the first cohort |

## 17. Build sequence

Twelve months to a defensible first cohort, and I would resist compressing it.

**Months one and two.** Verify supervisor eligibility per discipline. Obtain Ghanaian regulatory
answers in writing. Identify the anchor university partner and hold the first conversation with
their fieldwork or clinical education office. Convene the Ghanaian partner advisory board and
agree the payment model.

**Months three and four.** Draft the compliance and partnership packet in full. Build the
financial model including partner payments. Draft the scope of practice statement and the
emergency action plan, both with legal review. Confirm site profiles.

**Months five and six.** Write the competency framework with behavioural anchors. Write the
pre-departure modules and the facilitator manual. Negotiate the anchor affiliation agreement.
Recruit and orient the Ghanaian faculty who will teach the cultural strand.

**Months seven and eight.** Open pilot recruitment for a cohort of two to four. Train supervisors
on the evaluation instruments. Finalise the readiness gate assessments.

**Months nine through eleven.** Run the pilot cohort. Collect all evaluation data. Document
everything that goes wrong.

**Month twelve.** Publish the pilot outcomes report including partner benefit accounting.
Approach partners two through five with evidence rather than intention.

## 18. What I need from you

The signed or draft agreements with Ghanaian partner sites, plus the names and credentials of
every proposed supervisor. The current housing arrangement details, including location, security
measures, and cost. Any existing correspondence with any of the five universities, including
declines, since a decline usually contains the requirement you have not met yet. The required
hours figure you have already established, and which discipline and pathway it applies to. The
draft cost model. The list of potential clinical and community placement sites with the services
each provides. Any existing student facing materials.
