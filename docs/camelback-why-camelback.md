# Why Camelback? Four Answers

Camelback sets no word limit here and recommends a few sentences each. These
run 145 to 180 words, which is five to seven sentences per question, close enough
to the guidance to read as disciplined rather than as someone who ignored it.
Each answer is built with its load-bearing sentence first, so trimming from the
bottom never costs you the point.

Grounding facts, verified from Camelback's own pages: $40,000 unrestricted
capital, a dedicated 1:1 coach meeting weekly, a 16-week hybrid program, the
Expert Bench, the five core competencies (Be Strategic, Pitch Ready,
Stakeholder Development, Project and Budget Management, Hiring and Management
Teams), and their stated ethos of hustle over hype and humility paired with
ambition.

---

## 1. Why do you want to be a Camelback Fellow?

Camelback values hustle over hype, and that is exactly the discipline my
venture is practicing. I have served eight families and I am deliberately not
scaling until I can prove the report changes what a parent says in an IEP
meeting, which is a hard line to hold when most of the advice I get says grow
now and validate later.

What I want to learn are the two competencies I have never had to practice,
Stakeholder Development and Project and Budget Management. I spent twenty-one
years inside public education so I understand how school systems buy, and I
have never built an organization that does not depend on me personally.

What I want to challenge is the assumption that family advocacy is a
relationship business. The field runs on anecdote and individual advocates, and
I am building an instrument that scores an IEP against 54 conditions whether
or not I am in the room. I would rather argue that out with a cohort that
pushes back than keep being right on my own.

---

## 2. What will you bring to the #camelbackfam?

I train adults for a living. Twenty-one years across K-12 teaching,
instructional coaching, and educator preparation, currently designing
graduate-level special education licensure coursework, and I authored the
F.L.O.W. equity-centered coaching framework. Sharpening a curriculum,
structuring a training, or facilitating a hard conversation is my craft rather
than a side skill.

The more unusual thing I bring is fluency in how public education money moves.
Most education founders know their program and not the funding architecture, so
they price against foundations and never touch the money districts are already
required to spend. I can look at a Fellow's offering and say whether it is
allowable under Title I, Title II-A, IDEA Part B, or a WIOA set-aside, and
learning that changed my own model completely.

I also bring a high tolerance for being told no by institutions, which is what
twenty-one years inside them will give you.

---

## 3. What are you looking to get out of the Fellowship?

Capital and connections first, with coaching close behind.

The $40,000 solves a problem I can name precisely. My instrument is validated
on eight families, and no director of special education buys a diagnostic that
thin. Unrestricted capital lets me run it across enough families to defend it
in front of a superintendent, and buys down enough of my time to run an
institutional sales cycle while still delivering the service. Those two compete
for the same hours today, and delivery always wins, which is why the revenue
work never moves.

Connections is the harder bottleneck, because my revenue depends on district
central offices and a state vocational rehabilitation contract, and both are
relationship-gated in ways that do not respond to a cold email.

Coaching, because this venture cannot currently run without me and no part of
the audit is documented well enough for a second evaluator, which is the
difference between a practice and a venture.

---

## 4. How do you define the social impact outcomes you hope to achieve, short and long term?

Short term, I measure whether a parent raises a finding from our report in
their IEP meeting. I chose that over whether the district agreed, because
agreement was never in the parent's control or mine, while whether the parent
speaks at all is precisely what the report is built to change. I am running a
two-wave study on this now, and I will call the instrument proven at roughly
twenty to thirty families once the report reads clearly without a walkthrough
and raising findings in the room becomes a pattern rather than an exception.

Long term, every audit adds to a dataset on IEP quality broken out by district,
disability category, race, and income, and that dataset does not exist anywhere
in this country. Once it is large enough, a finding stops being one advocate's
opinion about one district and becomes evidence, which is what changes
institutional behavior at scale rather than one child at a time.

I will know we are getting there when a parent in that room does not need me at
all.

## The one call that is yours

The challenge paragraph in question 1. Camelback explicitly invited you to say
what you would challenge, and most applicants skip it or offer something safe,
so taking the invitation seriously sets you apart. It carries a small risk of
reading as combative. I think the risk is worth taking because the position is
genuinely yours and you can defend it in an interview, and cutting it is
reasonable if you would rather not open that argument on paper.
