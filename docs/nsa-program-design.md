## 1. What I reviewed, and what I am assuming

I reviewed two documents: the Updated NSAX Black in Rehab Proposal, which lays out an
eight week engagement with NSA, and the Workplace Excellence Academy curriculum, which
lays out a separate eight week workforce program. Both are strong raw material. Neither is
yet a curriculum in the sense that a second facilitator could pick it up and deliver it, and
neither yet contains the measurement design that would let you claim the program worked.

Four assumptions I need you to confirm or correct, because several recommendations depend
on them:

| Assumption | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| NSA is a school or center serving children with developmental disabilities, and the OT department is either new or informally staffed | Determines whether week 1 is a department build or a department improvement |
| The OT staff group receiving training is small, roughly six to fifteen people, and includes people who are not occupational therapists | Determines session design, whether inferential statistics are usable, and how much of the content must be interprofessional rather than discipline specific |
| Abigail is the internal OT lead and the person who will carry the systems after week 8 | Determines who owns the sustainability plan and who receives the deeper supervision investment |
| BIR is delivering this at reduced or no fee as a mission commitment, with replication to other sites as the strategic return | Determines how much of the budget goes to building reusable assets rather than to delivery |

## 2. The reframe that resolves most of the gaps

The proposal is currently written as a training engagement that also produces documents. The
work you are actually doing is standing up an occupational therapy department, and training is
one of the delivery mechanisms. That reframe changes several decisions at once.

If the deliverable is training, success looks like knowledge gain and satisfied participants,
and the natural evaluation is a pre and post confidence survey. If the deliverable is a
functioning department, success looks like referrals arriving appropriately, evaluations
producing usable findings, goals that are functional and measurable, documentation that
supports continuity of care, and supervision that continues after you leave. The second
definition is harder to hit and considerably more defensible, and it is the one that makes
this model worth replicating.

I recommend you rename the engagement to reflect that, something along the lines of "OT
Department Build and Clinical Capacity Program," and reserve the in-service language for the
individual sessions inside it.

## 3. Gaps in the current design

### 3.1 The sessions have topics, not learning objectives

Every session in both documents lists content. None states what a participant will be able to
do differently, under what conditions, to what standard. Without objectives you cannot write
an assessment item, you cannot brief a substitute facilitator, and you cannot tell a future
client what the program teaches. This is the single largest gap, and it is also the easiest to
close. Section 7 gives a worked example.

### 3.2 Two eight week programs occupy the same eight weeks

The NSA proposal contains four workforce sessions and one leadership session. The Workplace
Excellence Academy contains six thematic weeks covering substantially the same ground, with
its own pre and post assessment and its own set of participant deliverables. The two documents
assign different content to the same calendar weeks, use different instruments, and produce
different artifacts. As written, a staff member could reasonably ask which program they are in.

You have to decide whether Workplace Excellence is the workforce track inside the NSA
engagement, a separate product sold to NSA later, or a separate product sold to other clients.
Section 4 recommends a resolution.

### 3.3 The dose exceeds what a working clinical team can absorb

Counting the current design conservatively:

| Component | Sessions | Hours per participant |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clinical in-services | 4 | 4.0 to 6.0 |
| Workforce sessions | 4 | 4.0 to 6.0 |
| Case consultations | 2 | 2.0 to 3.0 |
| Leadership and check in meetings | 3 | 3.0 to 4.5 for leadership only |
| Baseline and post assessment | 2 | 1.5 to 2.0 |
| Total for clinical staff | 12 | 11.5 to 17.0 |

Eleven to seventeen hours of pull off the floor time in eight weeks, with week 4 carrying two
sessions plus a leadership check in, is a heavy ask for a small team that still has a caseload.
The realistic consequences are patchy attendance, sessions that get postponed, and a post
assessment sample that does not match the baseline sample. Protecting the time is NSA's
obligation, and it belongs in a written scope of work rather than in a verbal agreement.

### 3.4 Twenty five deliverables will produce five used documents

The weekly deliverable lists name roughly twenty five distinct templates, frameworks, and
guides, and the final deliverables list names thirteen. A department that receives twenty five
new documents in eight weeks will adopt a handful and shelve the rest, and the shelved
documents will make the adopted ones harder to find. Section 10 proposes a core set of eight
governing documents plus a clearly labelled resource library.

### 3.5 There is almost no implementation support between sessions

The evidence on training transfer is consistent and unflattering to the in-service format.
Didactic sessions alone produce small and inconsistent changes in professional practice
(Forsetlund et al., 2021), and transfer depends heavily on what happens after the session,
specifically on opportunity to practise, feedback on real work, and supervisory support
(Baldwin & Ford, 1988). Audit and feedback cycles, by contrast, produce small but reliable
improvements, and the effect is larger when feedback comes from a supervisor or respected
colleague, is delivered more than once, and includes explicit targets and an action plan
(Ivers et al., 2012).

Your two case consultations are the right instinct. What is missing is a repeated cycle: a
short practice assignment after every session, applied to real learners, reviewed with
specific feedback, then repeated. This is the highest value addition available to you, and it
costs facilitation time rather than new content.

### 3.6 The measurement plan measures the most convenient things

The baseline and post battery is clinician confidence, sensory knowledge, and workforce
wellbeing, with documentation and goal quality added at week 8. Three problems follow.

Self assessed confidence correlates poorly with observed competence, and the correlation is
weakest among the least skilled practitioners (Davis et al., 2006). A confidence gain is
evidence that people enjoyed the program, not evidence that practice changed.

Self report pre and post comparisons are also vulnerable to response shift, where participants
recalibrate what a given rating means as they learn more, so confidence sometimes falls while
competence rises. You can detect this cheaply by adding a retrospective pre item at post,
asking participants to rate where they now believe they were at the start.

Documentation and goal quality appear only at week 8, with no rubric defined, no baseline
chart sample pulled, and no second rater. Without a baseline pull in week 1 you have nothing
to compare against, and without a defined rubric two reviewers will not agree.

### 3.7 The sensory content carries evidence risk

Sensory processing is the most attractive topic in the curriculum and the most frequently
misapplied. Systematic review evidence supports sensory based interventions and environmental
accommodation more consistently than it supports sensory integration as a standalone
therapeutic approach, and the overall evidence base remains limited by small samples and
heterogeneous outcomes (Case-Smith et al., 2015). Your proposal already includes the right
guardrail in the line about individualization, and I would make it much more explicit: teach
staff to distinguish a manualized sensory integration intervention from a sensory informed
accommodation, and teach them what claims the evidence does and does not support. A program
delivered by Black rehabilitation professionals into a setting with limited access to services
cannot afford to be the source of an overclaim.

### 3.8 There is no facilitator layer

Everything in both documents is content for participants. Nothing is content for the person
delivering it. If the goal is a repeatable model, the facilitator guide is the product, and
the participant material is an output of it.

### 3.9 Data permissions are undefined

Chart audits, de-identified case presentations, and any learner level outcome data all require
a documented permission basis, a de-identification procedure that someone at NSA owns, and, if
you ever want to publish or present the findings, an ethics review determination made before
data collection rather than after. Decide this in week 1, because retrofitting consent is
usually impossible.

## 4. Resolving the overlap between the two programs

I recommend a two phase structure rather than a merge.

**Phase 1, weeks 1 to 8: OT Department Build.** Clinical content, department systems,
documentation, supervision, and competency. Four clinical in-services, four case
consultations rather than two, three leadership touchpoints, eight governing deliverables.
One workforce session sits inside this phase, covering sustainable practice and workload, and
it is there because burnout in this workforce is a delivery risk to the clinical build rather
than a separate topic (Maslach & Leiter, 2016).

**Phase 2, beginning four to eight weeks after Phase 1: Workplace Excellence Academy.** The
full six theme program, delivered as designed, to a wider staff group than the OT department.

This resolution gives you four things. The clinical build gets the room it needs. Workplace
Excellence survives as an intact product with its own pre and post battery, which makes it
sellable to clients who do not need a department build. The gap between phases creates a
natural maintenance interval, so the Phase 2 baseline doubles as a delayed follow up on Phase 1
clinical outcomes, which is the strongest evidence claim available to you. Phase 2 becomes a
second engagement rather than free scope.

If NSA insists on a single eight week engagement, cut rather than compress: keep the four
clinical in-services, keep four case consultations, keep one workforce session on sustainable
practice, keep the leadership sessions, and defer the rest of Workplace Excellence.

## 5. Revised curriculum architecture

Three strands, one spine.

**Strand A, Clinical Practice.** What OT is for at NSA, how learners reach it, how they are
assessed, how findings become goals, how goals become intervention, and how progress gets
documented and reviewed.

**Strand B, Workforce Sustainability.** Workload, regulation between clients, boundaries,
escalation, and the communication practices that let a small team hold a caseload. In Phase 1
this is one session and a set of leadership commitments. In Phase 2 it expands into the full
Workplace Excellence program.

**Strand C, Systems and Stewardship.** Referral criteria, documentation standards, supervision
structure, competency expectations, onboarding, and the ninety day and twelve month roadmap.
Strand C is aimed at leadership and at the internal OT lead, and it is what determines whether
anything survives week 8.

The spine that connects them is a single pilot cohort of three to five learners, selected in
week 1, carried through every session, and used as the applied material for every practice
assignment. Every framework you introduce gets applied to a real child that week. The pilot
cohort also gives you a natural documentation audit sample and a natural place to measure
learner level outcome.

## 6. Revised sequence, and why the order changes

The current sequence opens with sensory processing. I recommend moving it to the middle, and
the reason is substantive rather than cosmetic.

Sensory explanations are intuitive, memorable, and easy to over apply. If staff receive the
sensory frame before they have a reasoning structure for ruling explanations in and out, the
predictable result is that sensory becomes the default account of every participation
difficulty, and communication demands, task complexity, fatigue, environmental factors, and
skill gaps all get absorbed into it. Teaching the reasoning scaffold first, then introducing
sensory as one lens the scaffold can hold, produces better clinical reasoning and reduces the
overclaim risk in section 3.7.

The revised sequence also front loads the department infrastructure, because staff cannot
practise an assessment pathway that does not exist yet.

| Week | Strand A session | Applied work | Strand C deliverable |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Orientation and baseline. What OT is for at NSA, scope, and who we serve | Select pilot cohort. Pull baseline chart sample of ten records | Draft OT Department Practice Framework and referral criteria |
| 2 | Referral, eligibility, and the occupational profile. Gathering the story before the score | Complete an occupational profile and caregiver interview for each pilot learner | Referral and eligibility decision guide. Occupational profile form |
| 3 | Structured observation and activity analysis. Developmental versus chronological expectation, and assuming competence | Observe each pilot learner in one routine, write a functional problem statement | Clinical observation and assessment guide with an assessment decision tree |
| 4 | From findings to functional goals. Prerequisites, grading, and the just right challenge | Write two functional goals per pilot learner. Case consultation 1 | Plan of care template with the goal quality checklist built in |
| 5 | Sensory processing and regulation as one explanatory lens. What the evidence supports and what it does not | Complete a sensory observation for each pilot learner and revise one goal if warranted | Sensory informed practice guide |
| 6 | Beyond surface behavior. Identifying the function of a participation difficulty across sensory, communication, task, environment, and fatigue | Re-analyze one stuck case using the full frame. Case consultation 2 | Intervention selection guide |
| 7 | Functional independence across daily routines. Dressing, mealtimes, grooming, toileting foundations, play, transitions, fine motor, classroom participation, prompting and fading, home carryover | Implement one routine based intervention per pilot learner with a home carryover plan | Session note and progress monitoring tools |
| 8 | Documentation, reassessment, and knowing when to change or stop. Sustainable practice and workload | Post assessment. Chart audit of pilot records. Case consultation 3 | Reassessment and discharge guide. Competency, onboarding, and supervision handbook |

Leadership touchpoints sit at week 1, week 4, and week 8. Case consultation 4 is optional and
sits at week 6 or in a follow up month.

## 7. Learning objectives, activities, and resources: one session worked through

This is the pattern to apply to all eight sessions. Objectives are written at the level of
application rather than awareness, and each one has an assessment attached, because an
objective you cannot assess is a topic wearing a costume.

**Session 3: Structured observation and activity analysis**

*Duration:* 90 minutes, plus a practice assignment of roughly 60 minutes across the week.

*Prerequisites:* Sessions 1 and 2 complete. Occupational profiles finished for all pilot
learners.

*Objectives.* By the end of this session and its practice assignment, each participant will:

1. Conduct a ten minute structured observation of a learner in a naturally occurring routine
   and record what the learner did, what the environment demanded, and what support was
   provided, using the observation guide, with at least eight of ten required fields completed.
2. Break a target routine into its component steps and identify the step at which
   participation breaks down, producing a task analysis a colleague can follow without further
   explanation.
3. Distinguish a developmental expectation from a chronological expectation for a given
   learner, and state one prerequisite skill the learner has not yet acquired.
4. Write a functional problem statement in the form of learner, routine, observed breakdown,
   and suspected contributing factors, avoiding diagnosis language and avoiding behavior
   labels as explanations.
5. Decide, using the assessment decision tree, whether the observation is sufficient or
   whether a standardized or criterion referenced measure is indicated, and justify the choice.

*Activities.*

Ten minutes: open with two written problem statements from a previous cohort, one strong and
one weak, and have the group say what distinguishes them. This sets the standard before the
content rather than after it.

Twenty five minutes: teach structured observation and the observation guide, with a video or
live demonstration if consent allows and a narrated case if it does not.

Twenty five minutes: task analysis practice in pairs on a shared routine such as putting on
shoes or transitioning to circle time, then compare analyses across pairs and surface where
they diverge.

Twenty minutes: developmental versus chronological expectation, using two contrasting learner
descriptions, with explicit attention to assuming competence and to the harm that low
expectation causes.

Ten minutes: the assessment decision tree, and the practice assignment brief.

*Practice assignment.* One structured observation per pilot learner, one task analysis, one
functional problem statement per learner, submitted before session 4. Submission is the
assessment.

*Assessment.* Problem statements scored on a five item rubric: functional and routine based,
free of diagnosis as explanation, specific about the breakdown point, names contributing
factors as hypotheses rather than conclusions, and legible to a colleague. Two points per
item, ten available, eight as the threshold. Return written feedback within four days, because
feedback that arrives after the next session has no practice attached to it.

*Resources.* Observation guide, task analysis worksheet, assessment decision tree, two annotated
exemplar problem statements, a one page developmental expectation reference, and the rubric
given to participants in advance rather than held back.

*Facilitator notes.* The predictable failure is participants writing behavior labels as
explanations, for example "non compliant during transitions." Name that pattern early and
return to it. The second predictable failure is participants reaching for a standardized
assessment because it feels more legitimate, when the observation already answers the question.

Applying this pattern to all eight sessions is roughly two to three days of writing work, and
it converts the proposal into a curriculum.

## 8. Measurement plan

Organize outcomes by level rather than by instrument, using Moore's framework for continuing
professional development, which extends the familiar four level model into competence,
performance, patient outcome, and community outcome (Moore et al., 2009; Kirkpatrick &
Kirkpatrick, 2006). The point of the framework is that levels above declarative knowledge
require you to observe something rather than ask about it.

| Level | Question it answers | Instrument | Timing | Rater |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Participation | Did staff attend and complete | Attendance log and practice assignment completion rate | Weekly | Facilitator |
| Satisfaction | Was the session usable | Three item post session form, one open response | Weekly | Participant |
| Declarative knowledge | Do staff know the concepts | Twenty item knowledge test, identical items at both points | Week 1 and week 8 | Participant, scored by BIR |
| Procedural knowledge | Do staff know how to reason | Written case vignette, scored on a reasoning rubric | Week 1 and week 8 | Two BIR raters, blinded to time point |
| Competence | Can staff do it when observed | Observed structured task: conduct a ten minute observation and produce a functional problem statement, scored on a checklist | Week 3 and week 8 | Facilitator, checklist |
| Performance | Do staff do it in daily practice | Chart audit against a documentation and goal quality rubric, ten records at baseline and ten at post | Week 1 and week 8 | Two raters, blinded to time point |
| Learner outcome | Did children participate better | Goal attainment scaling on pilot cohort goals, plus caregiver report on one target routine | Week 4 baseline and week 8, ideally repeated at ninety days | OT lead and caregiver |
| System outcome | Did the department change | Referral volume and appropriateness, proportion of learners with a current plan of care, supervision sessions occurring, artifacts in active use | Week 1, week 8, and ninety days | OT lead |
| Workforce | Is the team sustainable | Selected wellbeing and professional confidence items, with a retrospective pre item added at post | Week 1 and week 8 | Participant |

Three design points that carry most of the credibility.

**Blind your raters to time point.** Strip dates from the chart samples, mix baseline and post
records together, and score them in one sitting. Without this, the rater knows which records
are supposed to be better.

**Define the goal quality rubric before week 1.** Six dimensions, scored zero to two each,
twelve available: the goal names an occupation or routine rather than a body function; it is
measurable; it specifies the context and level of support; the target is graded to be
achievable within the stated period; it carries a timeframe; and it reflects a stated family
or teacher priority. Publish the rubric to staff in week 4, because a rubric used as a teaching
tool improves practice and a rubric held in reserve only measures it.

**Do not run significance tests on a team of ten.** Report individual level change, the median
change, and the proportion of participants and records crossing a pre defined threshold, for
example the proportion of goals scoring nine or above out of twelve. This is honest, it is
readable by a school leadership team, and it is more persuasive than a p value from a sample
that cannot support one.

For the replication claim you will eventually want to make, the strongest available design
without a control group is a repeated baseline: pull chart samples from three months before
the engagement, from week 1, and from week 8, so you can show that the change coincides with
the intervention rather than with a general upward drift.

## 9. Deliverables: eight governing documents plus a library

Documents that govern practice should be few, short, and mandatory. Everything else is a
resource, and it should be labelled as one.

**Governing set, eight documents.**

1. OT Department Practice Framework, covering purpose, scope of service, service delivery
   model, caseload expectations, and the boundary between OT and other roles.
2. Referral and eligibility decision guide, including consultation criteria.
3. Occupational profile and caregiver interview form.
4. Clinical observation and assessment guide, with the assessment decision tree included
   rather than issued separately.
5. Plan of care template, with the goal writing framework and goal quality checklist embedded
   in the template rather than living in three documents.
6. Session note and progress monitoring tool.
7. Reassessment, program modification, and discharge decision guide.
8. Department stewardship handbook: onboarding, competency expectations, supervision
   structure, and clinical champion responsibilities in one document with three sections.

**Resource library, not mandatory.** Sensory informed practice guide, intervention selection
guide, routine specific strategy sheets, visual support templates, home carryover handouts,
professional development planning tool, and the clinical training materials.

**Workforce materials.** Keep these separate and label them as participant workbooks rather
than department documents. A department document governs practice and is enforced through
supervision. A workbook belongs to the individual and is not enforced at all, and conflating
the two makes both weaker.

**Handoff artifacts.** Pre and post findings summary, quality improvement priorities, and the
ninety day and twelve month roadmap.

That is eight governing documents, seven library items, and three handoff artifacts, against
roughly twenty five in the current draft. The reduction comes almost entirely from merging
documents that were split unnecessarily.

## 10. What NSA has to provide

Programs of this kind fail on the client side more often than on the consultant side, and the
failure is almost always unprotected time. Put the following in a written scope of work before
week 1, with a named owner for each item:

Protected, calendared time for every session, treated as non cancellable except for emergency.
A named internal champion with authority, who is not the person delivering the sessions.
Chart access with a documented permission basis and a de-identification procedure owned by NSA.
Selection of the pilot cohort by week 1 with caregiver consent for goal setting and outcome
tracking. A room with reliable connectivity if any session is remote. Leadership attendance at
all three leadership touchpoints. A commitment that the governing documents will be adopted as
policy rather than circulated as suggestions, since the competency framework has no force
otherwise.

Add one line stating what happens if these are not met, so the conversation in week 5 is a
reference to an agreement rather than a negotiation.

## 11. What to standardize so this model travels

The test of a repeatable program is whether a facilitator who did not design it can deliver it
at fidelity. That requires a clear line between what is fixed and what a site may adapt.

| Component | Standardized, not negotiable | Adaptable to the site |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Session sequence | The eight session order and the rationale for it | Calendar pacing, session length, and whether sessions are combined |
| Learning objectives | The objectives and their assessment thresholds | The examples used to teach them |
| Case material | That cases are real, de-identified, and drawn from the site's own caseload | Which cases, and the disability profiles represented |
| Practice assignments | That every session has one, applied to a real pilot cohort, with written feedback returned inside four days | Assignment scope, adjusted to caseload size |
| Governing documents | The eight document set and the required fields in each | Language, terminology, local regulatory references, and formatting |
| Measurement battery | The nine level structure, the goal quality rubric, blinded chart auditing, and the retrospective pre item | Which wellbeing instrument, and which learner outcome measure |
| Facilitator qualification | Minimum credential, practice experience, and completion of the facilitator orientation | Whether facilitation is single or paired |
| Fidelity checks | A per session fidelity checklist completed by the facilitator, and one observed session per engagement | Who observes |
| Client obligations | The protected time and adoption commitments in section 10 | How they are documented locally |
| Cultural and contextual framing | That the site's own staff shape it, and that examples reflect the population served | Everything about its content |

Three assets you do not yet have, and cannot replicate without:

**A facilitator manual.** For each session: objectives, timing block by timing block, the
common participant errors and how to respond to them, the required materials, and the fidelity
checklist. This is the actual product.

**An intake and readiness assessment.** A short instrument you administer to a prospective
site before contracting, covering staffing, existing documentation, leadership capacity, and
willingness to protect time, with a scoring guide that tells you when to decline. Declining a
site that cannot support the program protects your outcome data more than any curriculum
improvement will.

**A results template.** A fixed reporting format so that engagement three produces numbers
comparable to engagement one. Comparability across sites is the thing that turns eight weeks at
one school into a model.

One presentational note. The Workplace Excellence document marks nine separate activity names
with a trademark symbol. Marking every worksheet reads as promotional rather than
instructional, and it makes the curriculum look thinner than it is. Choose the one or two
marks genuinely worth protecting, most plausibly the program name and the framework, and let
the activities be activities.

## 12. Principal risks

| Risk | Likelihood | Mitigation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Attendance degrades after week 3 and the post sample no longer matches the baseline sample | High | Protected time in writing, recorded sessions, and a make up pathway that includes the practice assignment rather than only the content |
| Staff adopt sensory explanations for everything | High without resequencing | Move sensory to week 5 and teach the differential explicitly |
| Twenty five documents arrive and none is used | High as drafted | Reduce to eight governing documents adopted as policy |
| Confidence rises, practice does not change, and the report cannot tell the difference | Moderate | Chart audit and observed task, both blinded |
| The internal lead leaves and the systems lapse | Moderate | Build a second trained champion from week 1, and write the onboarding framework early rather than in week 6 |
| Findings cannot be published because permissions were not obtained | Moderate | Settle the permission basis and ethics determination in week 1 |
| Scope grows without fee because Workplace Excellence sits inside the same eight weeks | High | Split into two phases and two agreements |

## 13. What I need from you, and what happens next

**Send me, in this order.** The current baseline instruments as they exist today, including the
confidence, sensory knowledge, and wellbeing measures, so I can align them to the measurement
levels rather than rebuild them. Five to ten de-identified existing OT records or goal sets,
which will tell me more about where to aim the curriculum than any other single input. Any
existing forms the department already uses. Staff roster with roles, credentials, and years of
experience. The signed proposal or scope of work as it stands with NSA. The Workplace
Excellence pre assessment instrument. Confirmation or correction of the four assumptions in
section 1.

**First three deliverables I would produce.** Learning objectives and assessments for all eight
Strand A sessions, following the section 7 pattern. The measurement battery, including the goal
quality rubric, the chart audit protocol, and the knowledge test items. The revised week by
week program document with the two phase split.

**Decisions that belong to you before anything else moves.** Whether Workplace Excellence
splits into Phase 2. Whether the sensory resequencing is acceptable clinically and
commercially. Whether NSA will agree to the client obligations in section 10. Who owns the
pilot cohort selection and the consent conversation.
