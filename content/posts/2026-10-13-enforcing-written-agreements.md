You went to mediation, the district agreed in writing to provide compensatory services, and everyone signed. Three months later nothing has been scheduled, and the emails have gone quiet.

That signature created a legally binding agreement, and federal law gives you more than one way to enforce it.

## What the regulation says

Two kinds of written agreements come out of IDEA's dispute processes: a mediation agreement under § 300.506 and a resolution agreement under § 300.510. Both are enforceable in any state court of competent jurisdiction or in a district court of the United States (§§ 300.506(b)(7), 300.510(d)(2)).

Section 300.537 adds a path on top of that. Notwithstanding those judicial enforcement provisions, nothing in IDEA prevents a state educational agency from using other mechanisms to seek enforcement of such an agreement, provided the mechanism is not mandatory and does not delay or deny a party the right to seek enforcement in court (34 C.F.R. § 300.537).

## What the extra path looks like in practice

Many states allow families to enforce a mediation or resolution agreement through the state complaint process, which is typically free, requires no attorney, and moves on an administrative timeline rather than a court docket. Your state's special education complaint procedures, or your Parent Training and Information Center, can tell you whether your state offers this route.

The regulation builds in two protections while you use a state mechanism. The state cannot make that mechanism mandatory, and using it cannot delay or deny your right to go to court, so starting with a state complaint costs you nothing in legal position.

## A signed agreement is a commitment, not a favor

A district that signs a mediation or resolution agreement has made a binding legal commitment, and holding it to that commitment is routine enforcement rather than escalation. Start by writing to the district: name the agreement and its date, quote the specific term that has gone unmet, and state that you intend to enforce the agreement through the mechanisms the law provides. Districts that let an agreement drift often move once a parent shows, in writing, that the drift has been noticed and dated.

This post is general information rather than legal advice; a special education attorney or your state's Parent Training and Information Center can advise on the right enforcement route for a specific agreement.
