The front office calls at 11:15 and asks you to pick your son up early because he is having a hard day. No suspension letter follows, nothing appears in the portal, and by February you have made that midday drive nine times.

Federal law adds those days up, and past a certain point they become a change of placement with protections attached.

## What counts as a change of placement

Under 34 C.F.R. § 300.536(a), a disciplinary change of placement happens in one of two ways. The first is a single removal of more than 10 consecutive school days (§ 300.536(a)(1)). The second is a series of removals that constitutes a pattern (§ 300.536(a)(2)). The regulation counts school days, not calendar days, so weekends and breaks do not count toward the total.

## The three-part pattern test

A series of removals is a pattern when three things hold together: the removals total more than 10 school days in a school year (§ 300.536(a)(2)(i)), the child's behavior is substantially similar to the behavior in the previous incidents that resulted in the removals (§ 300.536(a)(2)(ii)), and additional factors point the same way, such as the length of each removal, the total amount of time the child has been removed, and how close together the removals fall (§ 300.536(a)(2)(iii)).

The district makes the first call on whether a pattern exists. The regulation assigns that determination to the public agency on a case-by-case basis (§ 300.536(b)(1)) and then makes the determination subject to review through due process and judicial proceedings (§ 300.536(b)(2)), so the district's answer is a position you can challenge rather than the final word.

## Informal removals count

The removals that reach the threshold rarely arrive as formal suspension letters. They arrive as a call asking you to pick your child up early, a shortened day imposed because of behavior, or a suggestion that he stay home tomorrow while things cool off. When the district initiates the time away from the placement because of behavior, that time is a removal, whatever anyone calls it and whether or not any paperwork follows.

Districts do not always log these informal removals, which is why your own record becomes the one that matters.

## A tracking habit that takes two minutes

Keep one running note on your phone, and add a line every time it happens: the date, how long the removal lasted (a full day, or the hours missed if partial), the stated reason, and who called or made the decision.

That log does two jobs. It converts a vague sense that this keeps happening into a count you can state precisely, and it preserves the details the pattern test asks about, since length, total time, and proximity are the exact factors § 300.536(a)(2)(iii) names.

## What crossing the line triggers

Once removals amount to a change of placement, the protections of § 300.530 attach, including the manifestation determination and required services, which an earlier post in this series covers. A change of placement is also the kind of decision a district must document and explain, and [our Prior Written Notice post](/news/prior-written-notice-when-school-says-no) explains how to ask for that documentation.

If your count shows more than 10 school days and the district maintains there is no pattern, put your log in front of the IEP team in writing and ask the district to state its determination and reasoning.
