The phone call says there was an incident at school, the school resource officer was involved, and a report has been filed. Your child has an IEP, and you are left trying to sort out what the report means for their education.

One short regulation addresses this moment directly, and it draws two lines worth knowing: what the school may do, and what the school must do with your child's records.

## What the regulation says

Under 34 C.F.R. § 300.535(a), nothing in IDEA prohibits a district from reporting a crime committed by a child with a disability to appropriate authorities, and nothing in IDEA prevents state law enforcement and judicial authorities from exercising their responsibilities under federal and state law with regard to that crime.

Read both halves. An IEP does not place a child outside the law, and a report to law enforcement does not place a child outside the IEP. The regulation holds those two facts together, which is exactly what a parent in this situation needs to do as well.

## The records rule and its limit

A district that reports a crime must ensure that copies of the child's special education and disciplinary records are transmitted for consideration by the appropriate authorities to whom it reports the crime (§ 300.535(b)(1)).

That duty carries a limit written into the next paragraph. The district may transmit those records only to the extent the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act permits (§ 300.535(b)(2)). FERPA generally requires parental consent before a school discloses education records, unless a specific exception applies. The two paragraphs read together mean the district is supposed to give the authorities context about the child, and every disclosure it makes must still fit within the same privacy law that governs all its other disclosures.

## What to ask for if it happens

Ask for the school's written account of the incident, including the date, what happened, who was involved, and who made the report. A written account you receive now is worth more than anyone's recollection at a meeting months later.

Ask, in writing, which records were shared, with whom, and under what authority. If the district relied on your consent, you will know whether you gave it; if the district relied on a FERPA exception, you are entitled to ask which one.

Keep your own dated notes of every call and conversation, and save every document you receive. If the incident also involves a suspension or removal, note the dates and length, because removal days carry their own protections.

## What does not change at school

A report to law enforcement does not pause or reduce the district's obligations under your child's IEP. Services continue, the IEP team's duties continue, and school resource officer involvement does not suspend any of it. If the school also removes your child from their placement, the discipline protections in §§ 300.530 through 300.536, including the manifestation determination under § 300.530, apply on their own terms.

A court process and a school process can run at the same time, and the school process keeps its own rules regardless of what happens in the other one. This post is general information rather than legal advice; if your child is facing a delinquency or criminal matter, a special education attorney or your state's Parent Training and Information Center can advise on your specific case, and any attorney handling the court matter should know an IEP exists.
