The school calls to say your son is suspended and a disciplinary hearing is coming. He has no IEP, so you assume the special education protections you have heard about do not apply to him. You emailed his teacher in October saying you believed he needed special education, and that email changes the picture.

Federal law extends IDEA's discipline protections to some children who were never found eligible, and whether your child is one of them depends on what the district knew before the behavior occurred.

## What the regulation provides

Under 34 C.F.R. § 300.534(a), a child who has not been determined eligible for special education and related services may assert any of IDEA's protections in a disciplinary matter if the district had knowledge that the child was a child with a disability before the behavior that precipitated the discipline occurred. The regulation calls this a basis of knowledge, and the timing is strict: what counts is what the district knew before the incident, not what anyone learns afterward.

Those protections include the safeguards that attach when discipline becomes a change of placement, such as the manifestation determination and continued services under § 300.530.

## The three ways a district is deemed to know

Paragraph (b) names three routes, and any one of them is enough.

First, the parent expressed concern in writing to supervisory or administrative personnel of the district, or to a teacher of the child, that the child needs special education and related services (§ 300.534(b)(1)).

Second, the parent requested an evaluation of the child (§ 300.534(b)(2)).

Third, the child's teacher or other district personnel expressed specific concerns about a pattern of behavior demonstrated by the child directly to the director of special education or to other supervisory personnel (§ 300.534(b)(3)).

Notice what the first route requires. A worried conversation in the hallway does not appear on that list; concern expressed in writing does.

## The exceptions

Paragraph (c) closes the door in three situations. The district is not deemed to have knowledge if the parent has not allowed an evaluation of the child, if the parent has refused special education services, or if the child was already evaluated and determined not to be a child with a disability (§ 300.534(c)).

These exceptions deserve weight when you consider declining an evaluation or refusing services, because that choice also sets this protection aside if discipline comes later.

## What happens when no basis of knowledge exists

If none of the three routes applies, the district may discipline the child the same way it disciplines children without disabilities who engage in comparable behavior (§ 300.534(d)(1)).

The regulation still leaves a path open. If someone requests an evaluation during the disciplinary period, the district must conduct that evaluation in an expedited manner (§ 300.534(d)(2)(i)). Until the evaluation is complete, the child remains in the placement school authorities determined, which can include suspension or expulsion without educational services (§ 300.534(d)(2)(ii)). If the evaluation finds the child eligible, the district must provide special education and related services, and the discipline protections in §§ 300.530 through 300.536 apply from that point forward (§ 300.534(d)(2)(iii)).

## Why this is the strongest argument for writing it down early

Work backward from a suspension. The question a hearing officer will ask is what the district knew before the incident, and the answer will live in whatever record exists. An email dated in October telling the principal or the teacher that you believe your child needs special education and related services is a basis of knowledge under § 300.534(b)(1). The same worry shared aloud at pickup is a memory two people will describe differently.

This series keeps returning to one habit because the regulations keep rewarding it: put concerns in writing, date them, and keep copies. If you go further and request an evaluation, a refusal obligates the district to explain itself in writing, which [our Prior Written Notice post](/news/prior-written-notice-when-school-says-no) walks through.

Nothing here requires magic words. A plain email saying you are concerned your child needs special education and related services, sent to the teacher or an administrator, satisfies the writing route. Send it the day the worry forms rather than the day the phone rings.
