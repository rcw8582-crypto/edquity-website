The progress report comes home with the report card. Next to each IEP goal, someone has checked a box marked "progressing" or written a sentence saying your child is working toward the goal. Nothing in it tells you whether your child is closer to the goal than they were in September.

Federal law requires more than that, and knowing what it requires changes what you can ask for.

## What the IEP already promised

Under 34 C.F.R. § 300.320(a)(3), every IEP must state how your child's progress toward each annual goal will be measured, and when you will receive periodic reports on that progress. Those two commitments are written into the document your team signed.

The reporting requirement is not satisfied by a checkbox. The IEP told you how progress would be measured, and the report is where the district shows you that measurement.

## The three questions a real progress report answers

Open the report next to the IEP and check whether you can answer these.

**Where did my child start?** The IEP's present levels section contains a baseline. A goal that began at 40 percent accuracy and a report that says "progressing" tells you nothing until you know the current number.

**Where is my child now?** The report should give you the same kind of measurement the goal uses. If the goal is stated in words correct per minute, the report should give you words correct per minute.

**Is that pace enough?** A goal is annual. If your child needed to move from 40 percent to 80 percent across the year and sits at 45 percent in January, the trajectory says the goal will not be met, and that is information you are entitled to act on before June.

## When the report has no numbers

Ask for the data behind it, in writing.

The IEP names a measurement method. Whoever wrote "progressing" used something to reach that judgment, whether that is work samples, curriculum-based measures, a running record, or trial data. Ask for the underlying data for each goal, and reference the measurement method the IEP itself specifies.

Districts are generally able to produce this, because staff collect it to write the report in the first place. What families discover is that the data sometimes shows less movement than the summary word suggested.

## Why a trajectory matters more than a checkbox

Endrew F. v. Douglas County School District RE-1 (2017) requires an IEP reasonably calculated to enable a child to make progress appropriate in light of the child's circumstances, and the Court rejected merely more than de minimis progress as the standard.

A progress report is where that standard becomes checkable during the year rather than after it. If the data shows your child moving at a rate that will not reach the goal, the IEP is not currently working as written, and the team can reconvene. You can request an IEP meeting at any time. You do not have to wait for the annual review.

## What to do with a report that shows too little

Request an IEP team meeting in writing and say why: the progress data for specific goals indicates the current services are not producing the rate of progress the goal requires.

Bring the reports and the baseline. A meeting where you can point to a number in September and a number in January is different from a meeting where you say you feel worried.

Ask what the team proposes to change. More of the same service delivered the same way is a decision the team should have to explain, given data showing it has not worked so far.

## The pattern worth watching

Progress reports that say the same thing every period deserve attention. A child who was "progressing" in November, "progressing" in February, and "progressing" in May, without a single number attached, has generated three documents and no evidence.

That pattern is common, it is fixable, and asking for the data is what fixes it.
