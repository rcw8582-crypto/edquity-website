# The IEP Quality Series: Six Self-Paced Modules for Educators

Edquity at the Margins · Curriculum design document · August 2026

Six self-paced modules that carry an educator through a full school year of IEP work, from the standard a document must meet through the co-taught classroom where the plan actually runs. The series repackages the five live professional development sessions and the forty-condition audit standard into learning a teacher can complete alone, which is what makes it sellable to individuals and licensable to districts.

Total seat time is twelve hours across the year, so a district can count the series toward professional development obligations and an individual pays roughly fourteen dollars per contact hour.

---

## The design problem self-paced learning creates

Live sessions work because you are in the room correcting scores, pushing back on a weak rewrite, and reading the confusion on someone's face. None of that survives the move to self-paced, so every module here follows four rules that substitute structure for your presence.

**Every activity has an answer key that explains the reasoning, not just the answer.** A learner who scores a sample IEP and disagrees with the key needs to understand why the reviewer scored it that way, since the disagreement itself is where the learning sits. Bare keys teach nothing.

**Every module ends in a product the learner keeps.** A rewritten goal, a one-page learner profile, an aimline chart, a signed pair plan. The product proves completion to a supervisor, gives the learner something usable on Monday, and gives you the evidence base for claiming outcomes.

**Every module includes a transfer task on the learner's own caseload.** Practice on sample documents builds the skill, though the skill only counts once it touches a real student, so each module closes by requiring one application to their own roster.

**Every module carries a facilitated extension.** The extension is what the facilitator package sells: the same content, plus the group protocol that turns individual scoring into inter-rater norming. A district buying the license gets the discussion structure you would have run yourself.

## What to launch first

Do not build all six. Release module 1 on its own as a paid standalone course, priced at $49, and let it tell you whether any of the rest is worth building.

Module 1 is the right first product for four reasons that hold together. It is already the shortest at ninety minutes, so the writing load is a weekend rather than a quarter. Every other module depends on it, which means the work is never wasted no matter what the sales tell you. It showcases the forty-condition standard, the asset no other provider has. It is also the only module that touches no student data at all, since the learner scores a synthetic sample rather than anything from a real roster, and that single fact removes the FERPA problem and the platform problem together.

**What module 1 alone requires.** Two synthetic documents rather than eight, and each one needs only its present levels and goals sections rather than a full IEP, because those are the only sections the ten-condition short form scores. The short-form rubric itself. One reviewer key covering ten conditions across two documents, so twenty keyed judgments with rationale. One fillable workbook. One video of roughly twelve minutes, which is optional for launch.

**Pricing at $49.** Ninety minutes works out near thirty-three dollars per contact hour, above the series rate of roughly fourteen, which is both normal for a small product and useful, since it makes the full series read as the value purchase when you eventually offer it.

**What tells you to keep going.** Set the threshold before launch so the decision rests on evidence rather than on how the first month feels. Twenty-five individual enrollments across a single semester, or three separate districts asking about a group rate, justifies building modules 2 through 6 and the platform underneath them. Fewer than ten enrollments says the demand is for live sessions and the self-paced format is not what this audience wants, which is worth knowing for the price of one weekend.

## The six modules and when to release them

Sequencing matters more than it does in live sessions, because a self-paced learner will otherwise take the modules in whatever order the list presents them. Release each one against the calendar pressure that makes it useful that week.

| Module | Release window | Seat time | Learner produces |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Compliance and Substantive Quality in the IEP | Summer, before writing season opens | 90 minutes | Scored sample IEP against the short-form rubric |
| 2. Data-Based Learner Profiles for Culturally Responsive Practice | Late summer, as rosters are assigned | 2 hours | One-page data-based learner profile |
| 3. Writing Present Levels and Measurable Annual Goals | Early fall, with the profile in hand | 2.5 hours | Three rewritten goals plus one repaired goal from their caseload |
| 4. Implementing Accommodations and Services with Fidelity | Fall, once implementation is underway | 2 hours | One-week service and accommodation map |
| 5. Progress Monitoring with Rate of Improvement | Two weeks before the first progress report window | 2 hours | Aimline chart and a rewritten progress comment |
| 6. Shared Planning, Instruction, and Assessment in Co-Taught Classrooms | Spring, in time to plan next year's pairings | 2 hours | Signed pair implementation plan |

## Module 1: Compliance and Substantive Quality in the IEP

**Why it comes first.** Every module after this one scores a document against a standard, so the learner needs the standard before anything else. Most educators arrive knowing compliance, because compliance is what monitoring visits check, and almost none have been taught substance, which is what determines whether a child makes progress.

**Objectives.** By the end of this module, the learner can:

1. Explain the standard set in Endrew F. in plain language, and say what it changed about the earlier reading that an IEP need only confer more than a trivial benefit.
2. Separate the twenty-one compliance conditions from the nineteen substantive conditions, and state what each type asks of a document.
3. Score a synthetic sample IEP against a ten-condition short form of the audit rubric, scoring each condition met or not met, and agree with the reviewer key on at least nine of the ten.
4. Name the three failure patterns they recognize most often in their own building.

**What the learner does.**

| Segment | Time | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| Compliance and substance | 15 min | Reading and short video: compliance against substance, and why a fully compliant IEP can still fail a child |
| The forty conditions | 20 min | The forty conditions grouped into six clusters, with one example finding per cluster |
| Guided scoring | 25 min | Score the present levels section of a sample IEP alongside a worked walkthrough |
| Independent scoring | 25 min | Score a second sample cold, then compare against the reviewer key |
| Reflection | 5 min | Name three failure patterns from their own experience |

**Self-check.** The reviewer key gives the score and the rationale for each of the ten conditions, so a learner who scored a condition differently can see the evidence the reviewer weighed.

**Facilitated extension.** The group scores the same document independently, then compares before anyone sees the key. Disagreement among colleagues is the discussion, and the facilitator guide supplies the norming protocol that turns it into shared standards rather than an argument.

**Evidence base.** Endrew F. v. Douglas County School District RE-1, 580 U.S. 386 (2017); Individuals with Disabilities Education Act regulations, 34 C.F.R. Part 300 (2006).

## Module 2: Data-Based Learner Profiles for Culturally Responsive Practice

**Why it sits here.** The profile comes before the document, because a present levels statement is the profile written into an IEP, and a teacher who has not built the profile has nothing to write present levels from. Placing this module after the goal-writing module would teach the sequence backwards.

An eligibility category tells a teacher what a student qualified under, never who the student is, and for high-incidence disabilities the gap between the two is widest. This module carries the mission most directly, since label-driven instruction is how students at the margins get taught to a category instead of to themselves.

Release it in late summer as rosters get assigned. A learner without a roster yet can complete every segment on the sample profiles and return for the transfer task once they know their students.

**Objectives.** By the end of this module, the learner can:

1. Read a full learner profile and connect cognitive scores, rating scales, and informal classroom data to what they observe in class.
2. Contrast label-driven and profile-driven instructional decisions for the same student, and name where the label actively misleads.
3. Use family and cultural context as planning data rather than as background narrative.
4. Build a one-page data-based profile for a student on their own roster.

**What the learner does.**

| Segment | Time | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| Two profiles, one eligibility category | 20 min | Two complete profiles sharing an eligibility category and needing opposite instructional responses |
| Reading the data | 30 min | What a standard score, a percentile, a rating scale T-score, and a curriculum-based measure each can and cannot tell a teacher |
| Label-driven and profile-driven decisions | 25 min | Eight instructional decisions, choose the profile-driven option, key explains what the label would have suggested and why it fails |
| Family and cultural context | 20 min | Where home language, prior schooling, attendance history, and stated family priorities change the plan |
| Building a profile | 25 min | Complete the one-page profile template for a real student, de-identified. This profile becomes the input for module 3, so the learner keeps it |

**Self-check.** A twelve-element completeness checklist for the profile, plus two worked exemplar profiles at different grade levels.

**Facilitated extension.** A grade-level or department team builds profiles for students they share, then compares the instructional decisions each teacher drew from the same profile, which surfaces how much variation a single document permits.

**Evidence base.** The disproportionality provisions at 34 C.F.R. § 300.646 establish the regulatory stake. Verify and expand the research citations here against your own reference list before publication, since this is the module where a district reviewer will look hardest at the evidence.

## Module 3: Writing Present Levels and Measurable Annual Goals

**Why it sits here.** The learner arrives holding the one-page profile they built in module 2, which is the raw material this module turns into a present levels statement and then into goals. Working in that order means the goals answer needs the profile actually established, rather than needs the writer assumed.

Present levels and annual goals are where your audits find the most failures, and every weakness in them travels downstream, since services get written to fund the goals and progress monitoring gets built to measure them. Repairing goals repairs the document.

This is the densest module, which is why the live writing session splits across module 1 and this one rather than compressing into a single sitting.

**Objectives.** By the end of this module, the learner can:

1. Write a present levels statement from a completed learner profile, connecting evaluation data, classroom evidence, and student strengths to the goals that follow it.
2. Diagnose the five failure patterns in weak annual goals: an unmeasurable target, an absent baseline, an unambitious criterion, a missing evaluation method, and a goal answering no need stated in the present levels.
3. Rewrite three weak goals so each states baseline, condition, behavior, criterion, and method.
4. Check whether an IEP's service grid, minutes, and accommodations actually fund the goals the document promises.

**What the learner does.**

| Segment | Time | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| Profile through goal to service | 15 min | Trace profile to present levels to need to goal to service in a strong sample, so the learner sees what alignment looks like before diagnosing its absence |
| Common failure patterns | 25 min | Twelve weak goals sorted by pattern, each with the audit finding that flagged it |
| Guided repair | 30 min | Rewrite one goal alongside a worked example |
| Independent repair | 45 min | Rewrite three goals cold, then self-score each against the five-point rubric |
| Service alignment | 25 min | Audit whether a sample's service grid funds its goals, and find the goal nobody is responsible for delivering |
| Transfer task | 10 min | Write present levels for the student profiled in module 2, then repair one of that student's existing goals |

**Self-check.** A five-point goal rubric with an anchor example written at every level, so the learner scoring their own rewrite has a concrete comparison rather than a description.

**Facilitated extension.** Pairs swap rewritten goals and score each other, then the group ranks a set of anonymized rewrites from strongest to weakest and defends the ordering.

**Evidence base.** Endrew F. (2017), on the requirement that goals be appropriately ambitious in light of the child's circumstances, plus your own audit findings on goal quality, which give the module something no other provider can offer.

## Module 4: Implementing Accommodations and Services with Fidelity

**Why it sits here.** The gap between what an IEP promises and what a classroom delivers is where families lose trust and where districts take on liability. By late fall the learner has a document they helped write and a week that does not match it, so the module lands on a problem they are living.

**Objectives.** By the end of this module, the learner can:

1. Distinguish accommodations from modifications, and state the general educator's obligation for each.
2. Translate a service grid and accommodation list into a specific instructional week.
3. Document accommodation delivery with fidelity a parent and an auditor would both recognize.
4. Coordinate delivery across general education, special education, and related services so nothing promised goes undelivered.

**What the learner does.**

| Segment | Time | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| Accommodations and modifications | 20 min | Fifteen sort items, including the several that change category depending on the purpose of the assessment |
| The general educator's obligation | 15 min | What the general educator must do, where discretion exists, and where none does |
| Mapping the service grid onto a week | 35 min | Map a sample IEP onto a real five-day schedule, including the points where minutes collide and something has to give |
| Fidelity documentation | 25 min | Three documentation systems weighed against each other on time cost and evidentiary value |
| Coordinating across providers | 15 min | Who else needs to know, and what happens when they do not |
| Transfer task | 10 min | Run the map on one student they currently teach |

**Self-check.** Sort keys with the reasoning on every contested item, plus a fidelity self-audit checklist the learner can rerun each grading period.

**Facilitated extension.** The group maps the same student across all the classrooms that student attends, which exposes the scheduling collisions and duplicated accommodations no single teacher can see alone.

**Evidence base.** IDEA implementation requirements at 34 C.F.R. § 300.323, on the accessibility of the IEP to every teacher and provider responsible for its implementation.

## Module 5: Progress Monitoring with Rate of Improvement

**Why it sits here.** Quarterly reporting is the moment IEP quality becomes visible to a family, and most progress reports say nothing a parent can act on. Release this two to three weeks before the first reporting window so the skills land the week the reports come due.

**Objectives.** By the end of this module, the learner can:

1. Set a data collection cadence that makes honest quarterly reporting sustainable on a full caseload.
2. Compute rate of improvement: set the aimline from baseline and annual target, then compare expected growth against attained growth.
3. Decide from the trend when progress is sufficient and when the data demands a program change.
4. Write progress reports that give families and the next teacher evidence rather than a routine mark.

**What the learner does.**

| Segment | Time | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| What a routine progress mark tells a parent | 10 min | Four authentic progress report comments, examined for what each actually tells a parent |
| Designing a collection cadence | 20 min | Probe frequency by goal type, built against a realistic caseload rather than an ideal one |
| Computing the aimline | 30 min | Baseline, target, weeks, slope: one worked example, then three to compute with a key |
| Reading the trend | 30 min | Six graphs, decide continue, adjust, or change the program, key supplies the rationale for each |
| Writing the report | 25 min | Rewrite two weak comments, self-score against the rubric |
| Transfer task | 5 min | Set the aimline for one goal on their own caseload |

**Self-check.** Computation keys showing the work, graph decision keys with reasoning, and a progress comment rubric with anchors.

**Facilitated extension.** The team brings real anonymized graphs and makes the continue-or-change decision together, which reveals how differently colleagues read an identical trend line and forces the group toward a shared decision rule.

**Evidence base.** Deno, S. L. (1985). Curriculum-based measurement: The emerging alternative. *Exceptional Children, 52*(3), 219-232. Fuchs, L. S., & Fuchs, D. (1986). Effects of systematic formative evaluation: A meta-analysis. *Exceptional Children, 53*(3), 199-208.

## Module 6: Shared Planning, Instruction, and Assessment in Co-Taught Classrooms

**Why it comes last.** Co-teaching is where students with high-incidence disabilities spend most of the school day, so the model deserves a full module, though it only pays off once the learner understands the document, the learner, the implementation, and the data. Release it in spring so a pair can plan the coming year, or in summer as the entry point for a schoolwide cohort.

Design this one for a pair to take together, since a co-teaching plan built by one teacher alone reproduces the problem the module addresses.

**Objectives.** By the end of this module, the pair can:

1. Establish a co-planning routine sustainable inside a real master schedule.
2. Select and rotate co-instruction models matched to lesson purpose rather than to habit.
3. Share assessment and grading ownership across both teachers.
4. Leave with a written pair implementation plan for the first grading period.

**What the learner does.**

| Segment | Time | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| How co-teaching drifts | 15 min | How a special educator becomes an extra set of hands, with the six warning signs that it is happening |
| Matching models to lesson purpose | 30 min | Station, parallel, alternative, team, one-teach-one-observe, and one-teach-one-assist, each matched to the lesson purpose it serves |
| Sustainable co-planning routines | 25 min | Three routines sized at fifteen, thirty, and forty-five minutes weekly, each with its agenda |
| Grading and assessment | 30 min | Who grades what, how modified work gets scored, and what the gradebook shows a parent |
| Navigating disagreement | 10 min | Scripts for the three conflicts co-teaching pairs actually have |
| Completing the pair plan | 20 min | Complete the implementation plan together and both sign it |

**Self-check.** A model-to-purpose matching key, plus a completeness rubric for the pair plan.

**Facilitated extension.** The building administrator reviews the completed pair plans and adjusts the master schedule to protect the co-planning time each pair committed to, which is the step that determines whether any of this survives September.

**Evidence base.** Murawski, W. W., & Swanson, H. L. (2001). A meta-analysis of co-teaching research. *Remedial and Special Education, 22*(5), 258-267. Friend, M., Cook, L., Hurley-Chamberlain, D., & Shamberger, C. (2010). Co-teaching: An illustration of the complexity of collaboration in special education. *Journal of Educational and Psychological Consultation, 20*(1), 9-27.

## Where the course actually runs

**For module 1, nothing new.** Take payment through a Zeffy ticketing form, matching the five that already serve the live sessions, which keeps fees at zero. The confirmation email carries a link to an unlisted page in this repository holding the video and the download links. Access control by unlisted URL is weak, and at one module priced at $49 the weakness costs less than any alternative, particularly since the materials contain no student data and no answer keys worth stealing beyond the course itself.

That defers the entire platform decision until sales justify making it, which is the point of dialing down.

**When the series earns its build.** Two paths, weighed once you have real enrollment numbers rather than now.

Thinkific on the Basic plan, at roughly $49 per month, gives scheduled release by date, downloadable file lessons, and completion certificates stating seat hours. Ten enrollments at the series price cover a year of it.

Building on the existing Supabase project costs nothing monthly and keeps learner work under the FERPA practices you already run, which matters specifically for modules 3 and 5, where teachers work with real student data. The cost is permanent maintenance: magic link lockouts, webhook failures, expiring storage links, all absorbed by you rather than by a vendor.

The deciding question is whether learners submit work for feedback. If they do, build it. If every transfer task stays on the learner's own machine, Thinkific is sufficient and cheaper.

**The facilitator license gets enforced by contract, not by software,** whichever path you take. Put the terms on the landing page and in the terms of service, then track district purchases in a spreadsheet until the volume justifies anything more.

## What you have to build before this sells

The curriculum above is the design. These are the assets it requires, ordered by how much they block a launch.

**A bank of synthetic sample IEPs, at least eight of them.** This blocks everything, since five of the six modules score, map, or repair a sample document. Write them as synthetic composites informed by your audit patterns rather than redacting real documents, because redaction rarely defeats re-identification in a small district and your FERPA posture on the audits is the reason schools trust you with real files. Synthetic documents also let you plant specific failure patterns where a module needs them.

**Answer keys with reasoning for every activity.** Roughly forty keyed items across the series. This is the single largest writing task and the one that most determines whether the series teaches anything without you present.

**Six rubrics and templates.** The ten-condition short-form rubric, the five-point goal rubric, the one-page profile template with its completeness checklist, the fidelity self-audit checklist, the progress comment rubric, and the pair implementation plan.

**Six fillable workbooks.** One per module, holding the activities and the space to produce the keeper artifact.

**Six short videos, eight to twelve minutes each.** Optional for launch, though they raise perceived value substantially at this price point and they let you carry the parts of your live delivery that text cannot.

**A facilitator guide.** Norming protocols, discussion prompts, and a slide deck per module. This is the entire justification for the higher-priced tier, so it should feel like a separate product rather than an appendix.

**A completion certificate stating seat hours.** Twelve hours across the series, listed by module, which is what a supervisor needs in a personnel file.

## Two things to settle before launch

**Professional development credit.** Tennessee educators need documented professional development for license renewal, and district approval of an outside provider varies. Ask your existing district contacts whether this series can count toward their approved offerings before you publish an hours claim, since a credible hours statement roughly doubles what an individual will pay and makes the district license far easier to justify. Until you have an answer, describe the series in contact hours without claiming licensure credit.

**Whether module 6 belongs in the individual tier.** Co-teaching only works as a pair, so an individual buyer gets little from it alone. Consider selling modules one through five as the individual series at a lower price, and holding module 6 for the facilitator package where a school can pair its teachers deliberately. That change would also sharpen the difference between the tiers beyond the license grant.
